Here's how Camus ends it.

The rock rolls down again. Sisyphus reaches the foot of the mountain and turns back toward it, one more time.

He knows it will never end, and he stops hoping it will. No rescue. No reward at the top. No next Friday. Giving that up is the price. And once he does, the rock stops being the gods' punishment. It's his. The struggle itself, Camus writes, is enough to fill a man's heart.

And he puts his shoulder to it again.

So did a sick man at his wife's family's table in Oran. The war wasn't ending. His lungs weren't healing. No steady job. He didn't wait for any of it to change. He finished the book.

"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

Not because the rock got lighter. Because once you stop waiting for a reward, the push isn't time you're getting through. It's your life.
