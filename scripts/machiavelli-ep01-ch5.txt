We know how he lived there, because he described it in a letter to a friend. He gets up with the sun and goes to his wood to argue with the woodcutters. After lunch he sits in the inn with a butcher, a miller and two brick-makers, playing cards, fighting over pennies, shouting loud enough to be heard in the next town.

Then evening comes.

At the door of his study, he stops. "I pull off my peasant-clothes, covered with dust and dirt, and put on my noble court dress."

And so dressed, he says, he walks into "the ancient courts of the men of old." He means his books, and the long-dead men who wrote them. He asks them why they did what they did. And in their kindness, he writes, they answer.

"For four hours I feel no weariness, I forget every trouble, poverty does not dismay, death does not terrify me."

Every night, a man with no office put on the clothes of his office, and held court with the dead.

And in the same letter, almost as an afterthought: out of those evenings he has made a little book. We call it The Prince.
