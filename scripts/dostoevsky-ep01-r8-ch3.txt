He asks for an answer quickly. His brother may have heard about the death sentence. He might still believe that Dostoevsky is going to die.

The letter moves between what happened that morning and what must happen next. There are debts. He needs money for the journey. He wants news of the children. He asks Mikhail to remember him to their friends.

Then he thinks of all the time he has wasted. The errors. The idleness. The life he did not know how to live.

“Life is a gift, life is happiness, each minute might have been an age of happiness. … I shall be reborn to a better thing.”

There is the promise. He has seen the end, and now the days beyond it seem possible again.

You know a smaller version of that certainty. After a frightening phone call, the person you were about to put off suddenly matters more than anything. You mean it when you promise to make time. It feels as if understanding what you could lose has settled how you will live.

But in this same letter, he asks his brother to write about the smallest family details. He wants the ordinary things. The things that could have waited yesterday.
