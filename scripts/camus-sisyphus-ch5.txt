Halfway down, and everything is going the way the gods said it would.

And the man at the desk? Camus has had tuberculosis since he was seventeen. The army would not take him. The war has cost him his job at a newspaper. If the gods are right about Sisyphus, they are right about him.

Near the bottom, Sisyphus is closer to us. Still not close enough to see his face. But close enough to see his step. And the step has not changed.

The gods were counting on the knowing to crush him. And that is not how a crushed man walks.

Here Camus turns the punishment over. A man who sees the whole of his fate, and still walks down to meet it, is above it. The knowing, he says, is the victory. On that walk, Camus writes, Sisyphus is stronger than his rock.

No god walks this hill with him. The rock is his, down to the last grain. The climb itself, Camus writes, is enough to fill a human heart.
