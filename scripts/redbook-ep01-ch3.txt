So what do you do with a minute like that? Most people take a holiday, or start a project, or pour a drink.

Jung did something stranger. On the twelfth of November, 1913, he sat at his desk, opened a small black notebook, and wrote to his own soul, the way you'd write to someone who stopped answering your calls.

"My soul, where are you?"

He tells his soul he's been away. Busy with the world, with patients, with ideas. He's back now.

But listen to the question he picked. Not "what should I do?" That's the question we all ask at the car door, and it wants a plan. "Where are you?" wants something else. It assumes something got lost, that it's still out there, and that it can hear him.

In the book he names two voices. One he calls the spirit of this time. It speaks in usefulness: results, value, what people will think. The other he calls the spirit of the depths. It doesn't care whether anything is useful. It wants meaning, and it says real meaning has room for the nonsense too.

Most of us only have a working relationship with the first one. It writes our calendar. The Red Book is what happens when a very successful man stops letting it.
