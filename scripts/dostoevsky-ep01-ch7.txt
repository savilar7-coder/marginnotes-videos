Years later, Dostoevsky writes The Idiot. Prince Myshkin sits with a family and tells them about a man who was condemned to be shot, then reprieved. This is a character telling a story inside a novel.

The man has about five minutes left. He divides them up: two to say goodbye to his companions, two to think about himself, one to look around.

In the novel, the man stands eighth in line. Dostoevsky stood sixth. The novel puts another man before the rifles and lets Myshkin tell his story to people sitting together in a room. They can interrupt him. They can ask what happened next.

He says farewell. He asks a friend an ordinary question and listens to the answer. With the soldiers waiting, he still follows what another person is saying. Myshkin does not tell the family what the question was. He tells them that the man was interested in the reply.

Then the man tries to settle what will happen to him when the soldiers fire. He has allotted two minutes to this. He is standing there, thinking, trying to finish before his turn comes.

He stares at a sunlit church spire. Light glitters on the gilding. He cannot tear his eyes away. In Myshkin's account, the man imagines becoming part of those rays of light. He keeps looking at them while the preparations go on.

He begins to count the minutes he would have if they let him live. He would keep account of every one. He would let none slip past. But the soldiers are still there, and he is still waiting. Finally, he wishes they would shoot him quickly and finish it.

Myshkin pauses. The family waits for him to go on. One of the daughters asks whether that is all. He says it is.

Then Alexandra asks about the man afterward. They gave him his days back. Did he keep careful account of his minutes?

“He said that he had not lived a bit as he had intended, and had wasted many, and many a minute.”

Dostoevsky wrote those words eighteen years after the square.
