Autumn, 1913. Jung is travelling alone, wide awake, when a vision hits him. He sees a flood cover Europe, from the North Sea to the Alps. Towns under water. Thousands of drowned bodies. Then the whole sea turns to blood.

Two weeks later he sees it again, worse. And he wonders, seriously, whether he is losing his mind. Was the flood about Europe, or about him? He didn't know yet. Either way, something was rising.

Now, here's why that scared him. He wasn't a lost man. He was thirty-eight, one of the best-known psychiatrists in Europe, with a family, a big house on Lake Zurich and a waiting room full of patients.

And earlier that year he'd lost his mentor. Sigmund Freud, the most famous doctor of the mind alive, a man Jung looked up to like a father, had picked him to carry on his work. That January, Freud ended their friendship, and most of their colleagues went with him.

Notice what broke first. Not the career. The map.

By map I mean the picture in your head of who you are and where you're going. Freud had been the man who told him who he was. Now there was no one.

In the Red Book he describes where he stood. He had won honor, power, wealth, knowledge, every kind of happiness he had wanted. And then the wanting stopped, and something like horror moved in where it used to be.

He reached the top of the ladder and found nothing up there he wanted. That's the car outside the house. Same minute, different century.
