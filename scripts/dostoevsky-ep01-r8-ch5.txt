Nearly two decades after the square, Dostoevsky returns to a condemned man's last minutes in The Idiot.

Prince Myshkin is visiting a family. He tells them about a man sentenced to be shot and then reprieved. This is a story inside a novel. Its condemned man stands eighth in line; Dostoevsky stood sixth.

Just before this, Aglaya has compared his way of valuing life to a rich woman who thinks only about how little she can spend. It is an uncomfortable comparison. If you guard every minute as a possession, when do you actually get to live in it?

The man believes he has five minutes. Two to say goodbye. Two to think about himself. One to look around.

For once, he can see all his time. He gives each part a purpose.

He asks a friend an ordinary question and finds himself interested in the answer. Myshkin never tells us what the question was. Something remains worth hearing, even here.

Then he tries to understand how a living, thinking person can become nothing. If something remains, what will it be? Where will he be?

He stares at sunlight on a church spire. He imagines becoming part of those rays.

And if they let him live? He would count every minute. He would let none of them escape.

The thought becomes so unbearable that he wishes they would shoot him quickly.

He wants all those days. He wants this waiting to end.

Myshkin stops speaking. One of the daughters asks if that is the whole story.

Then Alexandra asks the question the reprieve has left unanswered. The man got his days back. Did he actually live as he had promised?

“He said that he had not lived a bit as he had intended, and had wasted many, and many a minute.”

Alexandra takes this as proof: nobody can live while counting every moment. Myshkin agrees that he has thought so too. Then he asks why a person couldn't try.

Dostoevsky leaves the disagreement in the room.

The promise was sincere. His life did not match it. Did the insight change nothing, or had he asked it to change everything?

We cannot use this fictional man's answer as Dostoevsky's confession. But we can hear the question he allows Alexandra to ask. The man who writes about rebirth also writes a scene in which knowing the value of life does not make a person live exactly as he intended. He gives the objection a voice, and lets it answer back.
