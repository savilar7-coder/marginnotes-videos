Before he leaves Petersburg, they put irons on his legs. He travels to Siberia in a sledge, with a guard beside him. The chains weigh about ten pounds and make walking difficult.

On the journey, the sledges sink into snow. He gets out and stands in a snowstorm while they are freed. At Tobolsk, women whose husbands were sent to Siberia years before send the prisoners clothes and food. He has brought too few clothes. He receives more before the journey continues to Omsk.

Earlier, as they drove out of Petersburg, he watched the lit windows of the houses. They passed a house where his brother had told him the children would be attending a Christmas party. He looked at the windows as the sledge went past. Later, in his letter, he remembers taking leave of the children there.

There, he sleeps on bare boards in a crowded barrack. The men cover themselves with short sheepskins; their feet stick out. Prisoners wash their clothes in the same room. The floor is wet. Ice covers the windows. At night, the doors are barricaded.

The stove burns, but the ice on the panes does not thaw. Water drips from the ceiling. The men sleep with vermin in their bedding and get up in boots and thin sheepskins to go out into the frost.

He pounds alabaster. He carries bricks. He shovels snow. The irons go with him. When work ends, he returns to the barrack, eats among the other men, and lies down beside them again.

He eats bread and cabbage soup. When he has money, he buys tea and a piece of meat. He spends periods in hospital. When he manages to get a book, he reads it secretly.

The promise takes a page. The sentence takes four years.

A week after leaving prison, he writes to Mikhail. He asks for books, and for an answer.
