His book puts it as plainly as it can be put. If nothing out there hands you a reason, is life worth living?

Before he answers, he looks at how people try to escape the question.

The first escape is ending your life. Camus refuses it, flatly. And if that question is close to home for you right now, please pause this and talk to someone today. That matters more than anything else in this video.

The second escape is quieter. You tell yourself it will all make sense one day: in heaven, or in some big plan behind everything. You jump to that answer without proof. Camus aimed this mostly at thinkers who leap to God, and he's blunt: he calls it philosophical suicide. You stop asking the question by pretending it's already answered.

He has a plain word for what powers that leap. Hope.

Most of us do a smaller version every week. Next Friday will fix it. Next quarter. The next job. And Camus, at that table in Oran, had every reason to say it too. When the war ends. When my lungs heal. When I find work.

He takes neither escape. He keeps the question open, and he lives anyway. He calls that revolt. Not a riot: refusing to look away. What that looks like, he shows at the end, with the rock.
