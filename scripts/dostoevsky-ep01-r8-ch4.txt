Before leaving Petersburg, they put irons on his legs. He travels in a sledge with a guard.

They pass a house with brightly lit windows. Mikhail has told him the children will be there for a Christmas party. Dostoevsky looks at the windows as they pass. Years later, he will remember taking leave of them there.

In the Omsk barrack, prisoners wash their clothes in the room where they sleep. The floor is wet. The stove burns, but the ice on the windows does not thaw.

He lies on bare boards under a short sheepskin. His feet stick out. At night, the doors are barricaded.

He works in irons. He spends periods in hospital. The years returned to him have these rooms in them. He cannot choose a different life simply because he now values this one.

What would it mean to use every minute well here? To make the cold worthwhile? To find a lesson in another night without sleep? His promise has to survive days that offer him very little choice. We should be careful about calling a day wasted when getting through it is already work.

The promise takes a page. The sentence takes four years.

After prison, he writes to Mikhail again. He asks for books, and for an answer.

In 1864, Mikhail dies. In a later letter to a friend, Dostoevsky describes how impossible it feels to begin a new life after losing both his wife and his brother. More years cannot replace these particular people.
