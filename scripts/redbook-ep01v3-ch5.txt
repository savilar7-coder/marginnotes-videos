Jung doesn't fix the desert. He can't. He thinks about it. He explains it. He gets impatient with it. None of that makes rain.

The next part of the book is about waiting, and he hates it. He has spent his whole life being a man who acts, and now the only instruction is: stay. And his soul, speaking to him in the desert, is blunt about it.

"Can you not wait? Should everything fall into your lap, ripe and finished?"

As he admits in the book, no amount of cleverness makes a garden out of a soul. So he does the only thing left. He goes back to the sand, night after night, and stays.

Some things can't be forced, only allowed. Like falling asleep: the harder you try, the more awake you get.

We treat waiting as dead time between the things that count. In this book, waiting is the only work the desert accepts.

And notice where it happened. He didn't leave his family or move to a monastery. Same desk, same house, every night. His desert was a few steps from the dinner table.

So why does a hundred-year-old notebook matter at your car door? Because the spirit of this time got louder. Jung had newspapers, and less than a year after his vision, the First World War. You have a feed that never ends. Every empty minute gets filled before it can turn into a desert. Which means nothing gets the chance to grow there either.

Two honest warnings. Jung could afford his desert: money, a big house, his evenings. Most people's desert is ten minutes at six in the morning. That's fine. What counts is being honest with yourself, not how long you sit.

What Jung did next, inviting the visions back on purpose, like dreaming while awake, night after night, was dangerous, and he said so. These essays are a retelling, not a prescription. If the emptiness looks like depression, if you're not sleeping, not eating, not caring, that's a doctor's door, not a desert.

But if it's only the car-door minute, here's the smallest version of what he did. Tonight, ten minutes, no phone in the room. Ask his kind of question, not yours. Not "what should I do?" Ask "where did I go?" Write whatever comes, even if it's nothing. Don't fix it. Come back tomorrow.

You won't find a garden on the first night. Jung didn't. The first night is for finding out it's sand, and staying anyway.
